# 一句話結論

Local and state political opposition -- roughly 38 New Jersey bans, a New York statewide pause, a Texas interconnection freeze -- is a fast-growing but so far geographically diversifiable constraint, hard only at the level of a specific committed site.

# Executive Summary

A fast-proliferating wave of local, state and tribal opposition is blocking or delaying specific AI data-center sites, but not yet capping the national buildout. In the week of August 19-28, 2026 alone: New Jersey added four more municipal bans (38 total, most of any state); Indianapolis finalized a moratorium through 2027; the Cherokee Nation banned hyperscale projects on tribal land; and an Imperial County, CA court struck down a local moratorium. These sit alongside New York's statewide permitting pause (Executive Order 62) and a Texas freeze covering roughly 200 GW of pending interconnection requests. Data Center Watch estimates $64 billion blocked or delayed nationally over two years. Yet most state moratorium bills have failed, a court just overturned one moratorium, and federal policy explicitly avoids preempting these actions.

# Research Question / Why Now

**Primary question:** see front matter above.

**Sub-questions:**
- How large and fast-growing is the population of US local moratoria/bans and state-level restrictions on data centers as of August 2026?
- What do the highest-profile 2026 actions (New York, Texas, Indianapolis, Cherokee Nation) reveal about the mechanism and durability of this constraint?
- How much capital has been quantifiably blocked or delayed, and is the trend accelerating?
- Is this constraint hard (no substitute) or soft (developers can relocate), and does that differ at the site level versus the national portfolio level?
- What would falsify the thesis that this is a durable structural constraint rather than a reversible political moment?

**Why now:** A single week (Aug 19-28, 2026) produced four fresh, independent political actions against data centers -- New Jersey's 35th-38th municipal bans, Indianapolis's finalized moratorium, the Cherokee Nation's tribal ban, and a court reversal in Imperial County -- a distinct political/regulatory angle this pipeline has not yet examined, separate from its prior physical-equipment (transformers, copper, optics, helium) and technical/market interconnection-policy (PJM/BYONC) reports.

**Thesis:** Organized local, state and tribal opposition -- roughly 38 New Jersey bans, 70-100+ local moratoria nationwide, New York's Executive Order 62, a ~200 GW Texas freeze, Indianapolis's moratorium, and the Cherokee Nation's ban -- is a distinct, fast-proliferating "permission" bottleneck that cannot be resolved with capital alone, but because developers can generally relocate, it is structurally hard only at the level of a specific already-committed site, not at the national portfolio level, and its durability is already being tested by courts and failed state legislation.

# Known Facts

- Jackson Township became New Jersey's 38th municipality to ban data centers (~Aug 25-26, 2026), following North Brunswick, East Brunswick and Stafford Township the same week. _(evidence: ev-2)_
- Governor Hochul signed Executive Order 62 (July 14, 2026), pausing DEC discretionary permits for data centers of 50 MW+ until a Generic Environmental Impact Statement is complete (~1 year, no fixed deadline). _(evidence: ev-3)_
- Separately, the NY Legislature passed the Responsible Data Center Development Act (20 MW threshold, one-year moratorium) on June 4, 2026; as of Aug 24, 2026 Hochul had not signed it despite 60 legislators urging her to. _(evidence: ev-4)_
- Governor Abbott directed a PUC/ERCOT audit (Aug 3, 2026) of ~250-300 data-center projects representing ~200 GW before further interconnection proceeds; ERCOT missed its Aug 7 Batch Zero deadline in response. _(evidence: ev-5)_
- Indianapolis's Metropolitan Development Commission voted 6-0 (Aug 19, 2026) to bar new data centers until end-2027, exempting three already-approved projects. _(evidence: ev-6)_
- Cherokee Nation Principal Chief Chuck Hoskin Jr. announced (Aug 10, 2026) a ban on hyperscale data centers on tribal/trust land after ~64% of surveyed citizens opposed them; Seminole Nation and Kickapoo Tribe took similar stances earlier in 2026. _(evidence: ev-7)_
- Data Center Watch estimates $64B blocked/delayed nationally ($18B blocked, $46B delayed) over two years, tracking 142+ activist groups across 24 states. _(evidence: ev-8)_
- MultiState tracked 300+ state data-center bills across 30+ states in six weeks, and only 1 of 14 tracked statewide-moratorium bills (New York's) has succeeded; it estimates 70-100+ local moratoria nationwide. _(evidence: ev-9)_
- An Ohio ballot campaign to ban 25 MW+ data centers missed its July 1, 2026 deadline for 413,488 signatures and now targets a future election. _(evidence: ev-10)_
- Trump's Executive Order 14318 eases federal permitting but, per legal analysis, does not preempt state/local moratoria; Trump publicly criticized New York's moratorium (July 15, 2026). _(evidence: ev-11)_
- An Imperial County, CA judge set aside the county's moratorium (~Aug 28, 2026), finding it failed to show an immediate public-health/safety threat, the standard required for an emergency moratorium. _(evidence: ev-1)_

# Reasonable Inferences

- Because most state moratorium bills have stalled while local ordinances proliferate (38 in NJ alone), this bottleneck currently operates mainly at the municipal/county level, not through state legislatures. _(evidence: ev-4, ev-9, ev-2)_
- The Imperial County ruling suggests a meaningful share of quickly-enacted local moratoria may be vulnerable to the same procedural challenge if litigated rather than accepted. _(evidence: ev-1)_
- Federal policy explicitly avoiding preemption means near-term reversal is more likely to come from courts, legislatures or negotiation than from Washington. _(evidence: ev-11)_
- The $64B figure is small relative to total US hyperscaler capex over the same period, suggesting diversion of specific projects more than a national cap. _(evidence: ev-8)_

# Core Mechanism

Unlike a commodity shortage, political opposition cannot be resolved by paying more -- only by relocation, litigation, negotiation or preemption. That makes it behave differently by unit of analysis. For a developer's already-committed site -- selected for its interconnection queue position, water rights or fiber proximity -- a local or tribal ban is genuinely hard: there is no substitute for that site's accumulated advantages, and approval timelines are hostage to elections and litigation. At the national portfolio level, the same developer can redirect capital to another of thousands of US counties or non-moratorium states, which is why $64 billion blocked/delayed looks large alone but small against total US data-center capex. Durability is being tested on two fronts: courts (Imperial County shows rushed moratoria can be overturned) and legislatures (13 of 14 tracked statewide bills have failed), while federal policy chose political pressure over legal preemption.

# Bottleneck Analysis

The strict test separates a genuinely hard site-level constraint from a softer, diversifiable national one.

| Node | Demand | Supply elasticity | Substitutability | Pricing power | Opportunity | Classification |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Site-specific local/tribal permission | Extreme | Low | Hard | High | 3 | Structural Bottleneck |
| National portfolio-level buildout | Extreme | Low | Medium | Medium | 3 | Growth Area |
| Federal permitting/preemption layer | High | High | -- | Low | 1 | Neutral |

- **Site-specific permission** meets the strict test: relocating sacrifices interconnection position, water rights and fiber proximity that made the site valuable.
- **National buildout** fails the substitutability leg: thousands of counties and non-moratorium states remain available, consistent with $64B being diversion, not a cap.
- **Federal layer** is actively easing, not constraining, and does not offset state/local action by design.

# Strongest Bear Case

- **Economics**: $64B blocked/delayed is a small fraction of total US data-center capex over the same period -- friction, not a hard cap.
- **Supply**: Opposition is concentrated in specific corridors; developers retain substitutability by redirecting capital elsewhere, including most of Texas.
- **Execution**: 13 of 14 tracked statewide moratorium bills have failed to pass, showing weaker durability than the local-ban headline count implies.
- **Regulation**: Imperial County shows procedurally rushed moratoria are vulnerable to being overturned if litigated.
- **Competition**: Federal permitting easing and public pressure on states add a countervailing force, even without legal preemption power.

# Final Assessment

**Verdict: VALIDATE** (confidence: medium)

Local and state opposition meets the strict Structural Bottleneck test only at the level of a specific, already-committed site: extreme demand, low-elasticity approval timelines, hard substitutability once a developer has bet on a site's grid, water and fiber advantages. At the national portfolio level it is a Growth Area, not a hard cap -- $64 billion blocked/delayed is real but small against total US capex, most state moratorium bills have failed, and a court has already overturned one moratorium. This constraint is genuine and fast-growing, but its durability against litigation, failed state legislation and an opposed federal government (short of legal preemption) remains unresolved -- warranting validation rather than a stronger call.
