# 一句話結論

FERC killed PJM's federal AI-load curtailment plan on jurisdictional grounds, but the generation shortfall it was meant to ration is untouched -- authority just shifts to the states.

# Executive Summary

On October 9, 2026, FERC accepted PJM's Large Load definition and registry but rejected the Interim Resource Adequacy Service (IRAS) curtailment-priority framework and BYONC provisions, finding they intruded on states' retail-rate authority. This is the exact open watchlist event from this pipeline's October 1, 2026 report and meets an explicit "invalidate" kill condition set on the August 28, 2026 PJM/BYONC thesis -- but only for that specific federal policy mechanism. PJM's underlying firm-generation shortfall, reaffirmed independently as a third-consecutive-year auction shortfall, is unaffected. Analysts expect curtailment-priority authority to move to individual states, led by Pennsylvania and Ohio, while a separate $29.4 billion question of who pays for data-center-driven capacity costs remains open.

# Known Facts

- Oct 9, 2026: FERC accepted PJM's 50 MW Large Load definition and registry (effective Oct 12) but rejected the IRAS curtailment framework and BYONC provisions. _(s1, s2, s3, s5)_
- FERC's reasoning: IRAS obligations fell on retail customers and its compensation framework resembled a state-reserved retail rate. _(s5, s6, s7)_
- BYONC and related capacity-market changes were rejected as inextricably linked to IRAS, not severable. _(s7, s9)_
- Cited as 197 FERC ¶ 61,019, with a concurrence from Commissioner Rosner, who said curtailment priority is a state matter. _(s4, s9)_
- PJM said it is reviewing the order to meet FERC's requirements while recognizing states' role; declined to detail next steps. _(s7, s10)_
- Market reaction was muted; most stakeholders already expected states, led by Pennsylvania and Ohio, to set their own rules using PJM's new registry data. _(s11, s9)_
- PJM's own Independent Market Monitor said data centers drove $29.4B (46%) of PJM's last four auctions' capacity charges, $6.3B (~38%) in the latest alone; this order left that allocation unresolved. _(s13, s14)_

# Reasonable Inferences

- FERC rejected IRAS/BYONC on jurisdiction, not on finding curtailment unnecessary -- it most directly kills this federal vehicle while inviting a state or refiled successor. _(s5, s6, s7, s9)_
- Because generation scarcity is untouched, the Structural Bottleneck classification for firm PJM capacity should not change based on this order alone. _(s1, s13)_
- Because multiple states may act at different paces, the practical effect looks like fragmenting one rationing mechanism into up to 13 inconsistent state processes rather than eliminating one. _(s9, s11)_

# Core Mechanism

FERC's capacity-market oversight requires that any mechanism forcing specific customers to bear curtailment risk or cost be shown "just and reasonable" and not an improper reach into retail ratemaking, which the Federal Power Act reserves to states. PJM's IRAS/BYONC package asked FERC to approve exactly that kind of customer-facing obligation at the federal level; FERC held it crossed that line, severing it from the otherwise-approved data-gathering registry.

That does not make the underlying shortfall disappear -- it relocates the question of who gets curtailed first, and at what cost, to roughly a dozen separate state proceedings instead of one. States can move at different speeds and reach different answers, which is a different kind of constraint (slower, less uniform) than a single rejected federal rule, but not obviously a smaller one for a developer trying to plan a multi-state buildout.

# Final Assessment

**Verdict: VALIDATE** (confidence: medium)

FERC's order meets the letter of an "invalidate" kill condition for the specific federal BYONC/IRAS mechanism this pipeline flagged in August, but the deeper thesis -- that firm generation capacity, not policy design, is PJM's real constraint -- stands unshaken, since the ruling never touched the generation shortfall itself. What changes is who decides the rationing rules: a single federal docket gives way to a slower, less uniform set of state processes, with Pennsylvania and Ohio likely first movers and a $29.4 billion cost question still unresolved. The validation events here, especially any PJM refiling and the first state actions, should clarify within one to two quarters whether this becomes a workable patchwork or a prolonged vacuum.
