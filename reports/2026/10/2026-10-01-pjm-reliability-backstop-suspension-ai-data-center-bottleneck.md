# 一句話結論

FERC suspended PJM's backstop power auction over cost disputes, delaying -- not resolving -- the capacity shortfall and leaving the AI-load curtailment rules undecided.

# Executive Summary

On September 29, 2026, FERC accepted but suspended for five months PJM's Reliability Backstop Procurement, the one-time auction meant to close a 6,831 MW reserve-margin shortfall, over unresolved cost-allocation and collateral issues. PJM immediately canceled its planned September 30-October 21, 2026 auction. This directly engages a watchlist event from this pipeline's August 28, 2026 report, but the curtailment-priority (BYONC) framework that report centered on actually sits in a separate, still-undecided docket, protested by both PJM's own market monitor and power suppliers. The underlying scarcity -- a third consecutive capacity-auction shortfall -- remains real; the regulatory process meant to address it has become its own source of delay.

# Known Facts

- Sept 29, 2026: FERC accepted PJM's Reliability Backstop Procurement (ER26-3380) in part but suspended it five months to Feb 28, 2027, pending a hearing on cost allocation, transmission-owner-exit rules, and collateral requirements. _(s1, s2, s4)_
- FERC Chair Laura Swett said PJM's rushed filing left no time "to rehabilitate the mess we received." _(s2)_
- PJM suspended its planned Sept 30-Oct 21, 2026 backstop auction, which targeted the 6,831 MW shortfall from its July 14, 2026 capacity auction. _(s3, s4, s5)_
- That July auction procured 138,318 MW UCAP at the $325/MW-day price cap, a 5.6% reserve-margin shortfall -- the third consecutive shortfall year. _(s10, s11)_
- PJM's companion IRAS/BYONC curtailment-priority filing (ER26-3515, filed Aug 13, 2026, requested effective Oct 12, 2026) remains undecided by FERC. _(s14, s8)_
- PJM's own Independent Market Monitor called that filing "a convoluted, inconsistent, confusing proposal" and urged rejection; EPSA separately protested BYONC as anti-competitive toward existing generators. _(s8, s9)_
- Maryland's Office of People's Counsel estimated up to $483M (Potomac Edison) and $79M (BGE) in 15-year ratepayer exposure from the backstop auction. _(s6, s7)_
- Constellation Energy shares fell ~4.9% on the order; NRG and Talen also declined, with TD Cowen citing "acute" uncertainty. _(s12, s13)_

# Reasonable Inferences

- Because FERC's order addressed only the capacity-auction docket and not the curtailment-priority filing central to the August thesis, that watchlist event has only partially resolved -- the procurement path is delayed while the rationing rules remain contested. _(s1, s8, s9, s14)_
- Because opposition comes simultaneously from consumer advocates (too costly) and power suppliers (anti-competitive), a materially watered-down final framework looks at least as likely as approval largely as filed. _(s6, s7, s8, s9)_
- Because the shortfall (5.6%) held roughly steady while the clearing price edged down from last year's cap, physical scarcity looks stable rather than accelerating; the new five-month suspension is, for now, the bigger added delay. _(s10, s11, s1)_

# Core Mechanism

PJM's capacity market is meant to signal scarcity through price, letting generators and now large loads respond; when the ordinary auction clears short three years running, PJM's backup plan is a one-time emergency procurement to force new supply into the gap quickly. FERC's suspension shows that even an emergency mechanism must still satisfy ordinary "just and reasonable" cost-allocation standards -- and that satisfying them, amid simultaneous objections from ratepayer advocates and competing generators, takes months FERC itself calls avoidable only if PJM moves fast.

The curtailment-priority (BYONC) piece that actually rations access during a shortfall sits in a separate, slower-moving docket, protested from two directions at once. Until both dockets resolve, PJM's AI-load developers face uncertainty not over whether capacity is scarce -- the auction data already confirms that -- but over exactly which costs and which curtailment risks attach to which projects.

# Strongest Bear Case

- **Regulation**: FERC explicitly encouraged a quick refiling that could avoid the hearing, suggesting this is a correctable procedural snag, not a structural rejection.
- **Economics**: The shortfall (5.6%) and clearing price ($325) both came in slightly better than the prior year's cap, so scarcity is not visibly worsening each cycle.
- **Competition**: EPSA's protest is about fairness to existing generators, not physical supply -- a different problem than a pure shortage.
- **Demand**: Slower AI capex growth could ease pressure on these dockets independent of how FERC rules.
- **Execution**: Well-capitalized developers already have a working BYONC workaround, so delay may bind smaller players more than the largest ones.

# Final Assessment

**Verdict: VALIDATE** (confidence: medium)

FERC's order neither confirms nor kills the August 2026 thesis: it delays a related capacity-procurement docket over cost disputes while leaving the actual curtailment-priority framework pending in a separate, contested docket. The underlying scarcity is real and persistent -- a third straight shortfall year -- but the regulatory path to addressing it has itself become a source of delay, squeezed between consumer advocates and power suppliers. The validation events set here, especially FERC's still-pending IRAS/BYONC order, should clarify within one to two quarters whether this resolves into a working framework or a protracted standoff.
