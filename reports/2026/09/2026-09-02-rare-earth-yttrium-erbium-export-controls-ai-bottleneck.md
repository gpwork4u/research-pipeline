# 一句話結論

China's near-monopoly on yttrium and erbium processing is squeezing gas-turbine and data-center optical-networking supply chains, but the restriction is a reversible trade-truce artifact expiring November 10, 2026, not a physical shortage.

# Executive Summary

China's October and November 2025 export-control actions on yttrium and erbium -- rare earths feeding gas-turbine thermal barrier coatings and the erbium-doped fiber amplifiers that carry AI data-center network traffic -- have cut non-China yttrium exports by roughly 75% year-on-year and pushed European erbium prices up more than 50% since June 2026. GE Vernova has drawn down stockpiles to keep turbines running; ex-China prices for these materials run several multiples above China's own domestic prices. Yet the binding constraint traces to a negotiated, time-limited suspension expiring November 10, 2026, not to physical scarcity -- China has favored targeted blacklisting (MP Materials, USA Rare Earth) over blanket cutoffs, and non-China separation capacity is only now commissioning. The key uncertainty is Beijing's November decision.

# Research Question / Why Now

**Primary question:** see front matter above.

**Sub-questions:**
- What do China's October 2025 and November 2025 export-control announcements cover for yttrium and erbium, and what is their status today?
- How much has non-China supply actually fallen, and what do prices show?
- Which AI-adjacent industries -- gas turbines and optical networking -- are most exposed, and what have they disclosed?
- Is there a viable near-term substitute for erbium in fiber amplification or yttrium in turbine coatings?
- What would falsify the thesis that this is a durable structural constraint rather than a reversible trade-negotiation artifact?

**Why now:** In the week of August 28-September 2, 2026, The Register and IEEE Spectrum reported 2026 yttrium exports down roughly 75% year-on-year, the Financial Times reported European erbium prices up over 50% since June, and analysts flagged November 10, 2026 as the deadline for China's decision on whether to extend, narrow or let its rare-earth export-control suspension lapse -- a distinct materials-supply angle not yet examined by this pipeline, feeding into the gas-turbine power and optical-networking supply chains already covered from other angles.

**Thesis:** China's control of yttrium and erbium processing has created a materials-level bottleneck touching two AI-buildout-critical supply chains -- gas-turbine power (yttria thermal barrier coatings) and data-center optical networking (erbium-doped fiber amplifiers) -- with high demand, low supply elasticity, and hard near-term substitutability for the highest-purity applications. Its durability is contested: it stems from a negotiated trade truce rather than physical scarcity, China favors calibrated targeted measures over blanket cutoffs, and Western stockpiling and diversification are already underway ahead of the November 2026 deadline.

# Known Facts

- MOFCOM Announcement No. 18 of 2025 (Oct 9, 2025) added seven medium/heavy rare earths, including yttrium, to China's controlled-export list, requiring licenses for exporters and buyers; it has applied without interruption. _(evidence: ev-3)_
- MOFCOM/GACC Announcement No. 70 of 2025 (Nov 7, 2025) suspended a second wave of controls -- covering holmium, erbium, thulium, europium and ytterbium -- for twelve months, until November 10, 2026. _(evidence: ev-4)_
- 2026 yttrium exports to non-China markets are down roughly 75% year-on-year; China mines ~90% and processes essentially all globally usable yttrium oxide. _(evidence: ev-1)_
- Yttrium feeds yttria-stabilized turbine thermal barrier coatings and small quantities in multi-layer ceramic capacitors used in AI server electronics. _(evidence: ev-1)_
- GE Vernova's CEO said at its December 2025 investor day the company held enough yttrium to last into 2026 and is working with the US government on additional supply. _(evidence: ev-6)_
- The Financial Times reported European erbium prices up over 50% since June 2026 amid stockpiling ahead of the November 10 deadline. _(evidence: ev-9)_
- China blacklisted MP Materials and USA Rare Earth on June 22, 2026, using targeted company-specific measures rather than reimposing broad controls. _(evidence: ev-9)_
- Rare-earth pricing has split two-tier: dysprosium trades at $205-211/kg in China versus $575-2,500+/kg ex-China; yttrium oxide reached near $1,100/kg ex-China by May 2026, up from under $8/kg pre-restriction. _(evidence: ev-8)_
- The 2026 USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries state the US holds no rare-earth stockpile and remains substantially import-reliant for yttrium. _(evidence: ev-5)_
- MP Materials is commissioning heavy-rare-earth separation capacity (200 tonnes/year Dy+Tb) in mid-2026; Lynas signed a $96 million, four-year DoD supply agreement (Mar 16, 2026); USA Rare Earth's Round Top project targets yttrium and erbium among twelve minerals. _(evidence: ev-11)_
- Semiconductor optical amplifiers are a technically viable EDFA substitute but remain commercially uncompetitive on noise and performance as of 2026. _(evidence: ev-10)_

# Reasonable Inferences

- Because the erbium restriction is a negotiated suspension expiring Nov 10, 2026, this bottleneck is more directly reversible by policy decision than mining- or manufacturing-capacity-constrained bottlenecks this pipeline has previously examined. _(evidence: ev-4, ev-9)_
- China's targeted blacklisting of MP Materials and USA Rare Earth, rather than a blanket cutoff, suggests Beijing currently prefers calibrated leverage over its rivals' substitution response to broader escalation. _(evidence: ev-9)_
- The wide China-domestic-versus-ex-China price gap indicates Western buyers are paying a supply-security premium independent of physical scarcity inside China. _(evidence: ev-8)_
- Because gas-turbine backlogs and optical-networking equipment both sit downstream of these materials, a sustained constraint would compound, not create independently, delay risk already present from other bottlenecks. _(evidence: ev-1, ev-6)_

# Core Mechanism

Yttrium and erbium reach AI-adjacent supply chains through two separate paths that share a single upstream chokepoint: China's near-total control of separating raw rare-earth ore into industrially usable oxides and metals. Yttrium stabilizes the ceramic thermal barrier coatings that let gas turbines run at AI-data-center-relevant temperatures; erbium is the gain medium inside the fiber amplifiers that boost optical signals across data-center-interconnect and backbone networks. Because China mines roughly 90% of yttrium and processes essentially all globally usable output, and because the highest-purity, highest-reliability grades used in turbine coatings and amplifier fiber lack a commercially competitive substitute today, demand growth is high and near-term substitutability is hard. What separates this from a conventional supply bottleneck is that the acute restriction -- on erbium and four related elements -- is a negotiated suspension tied to a bilateral trade truce, not a mine-to-market capacity constraint; its expiration date, not a construction timeline, is the operative clock. China's willingness to use narrower, company-specific tools (blacklisting the two firms most likely to erode its leverage) rather than broad cutoffs signals calibrated pressure rather than an intent toward permanent decoupling, even as buyers hedge with stockpiles and price premiums.

# Bottleneck Analysis

The strict test isolates the genuinely hard chokepoint from layers with more room to adapt.

| Node | Demand | Supply elasticity | Substitutability | Pricing power | Opportunity | Classification |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chinese processing of yttrium/erbium | High | Low | Hard | High | 3 | Structural Bottleneck |
| Non-China downstream fabrication | High | Medium | Medium | Medium | 2 | Growth Area |
| Domestic/allied alternative supply chain | High | Low | -- | Low | 3 | Neutral |

- **Upstream processing** meets the strict test: high demand, low elasticity from China's near-total share, hard substitutability for top-tier applications.
- **Downstream fabrication** has more elasticity via stockpiling and diversified sourcing, softening but not eliminating exposure.
- **Alternative supply** is the intended fix but remains nascent and is itself a direct target of Chinese trade leverage.

# Strongest Bear Case

- **Regulation**: The core restriction is a negotiated, time-limited suspension, not a physical shortage; an extension or easing on November 10 would substantially defuse it.
- **Supply**: Non-China separation capacity is already commissioning in 2026, unlike bottlenecks with far longer lead times such as copper mine permitting.
- **Demand**: Yttrium's AI-relevant uses are a modest share of total demand dominated by lighting phosphors and ceramics, diluting AI-specific price pressure.
- **Technology**: Optical amplifiers and recycling both offer partial substitution paths that could mature faster than expected.
- **Economics**: The wide China-domestic-vs-ex-China price gap suggests scarcity is substantially a licensing-and-logistics artifact that could unwind quickly if controls ease.

# Final Assessment

**Verdict: VALIDATE** (confidence: medium)

Yttrium and erbium processing meets the strict Structural Bottleneck test at the China-processing layer: demand is high, supply elasticity is low given China's near-total processing share, and substitutability is hard for the highest-purity turbine-coating and fiber-amplifier applications. But this constraint is unusually reversible by comparison with this pipeline's prior physical-capacity bottlenecks -- it is anchored to a trade-truce suspension expiring November 10, 2026, China has so far favored calibrated targeted measures over blanket cutoffs, and non-China alternative capacity, while nascent, is already commissioning. That mix of genuine near-term exposure and a concrete, near-dated resolution point warrants validation rather than a stronger call in either direction.
