# One-Sentence Conclusion

Long-haul interstate gas pipelines, not turbines or the grid, are AI data centers' next power bottleneck, but faster laterals and FERC reform blunt it.

# Executive Summary

Williams, Kinder Morgan and Energy Transfer each disclosed record or near-record 2026 project backlogs explicitly tied to AI data-center and power demand: Williams closed a $5.5B Haynesville acquisition and raised its long-term growth target; Kinder Morgan reported 60%+ of its $9.6B backlog tied to power/utility demand; Energy Transfer disclosed 6+ Bcf/d of contracted data-center capacity. Yet flagship interstate expansions take years -- Williams' 950 MMcf/d Transco Power Express is not targeted online until July 2030 -- while a pre-reform FERC certificate backlog averaged 465 days. FERC's unanimous May 2026 rulemaking directly targets that permitting delay, and capital-light laterals bypassing certificate review have shipped in a single quarter. No hyperscaler has yet blamed a delay on pipeline capacity specifically, and analyst estimates of real incremental gas demand span a wide 3-20 Bcf/d by 2030.

# Research Question / Why Now

**Primary question:** see front matter above.

**Sub-questions:** How much backlog growth is tied to data centers, and when does it come online? Does the multi-year certification/construction timeline constitute a bottleneck distinct from gas turbines? How much faster do laterals move than mainline expansions? Does FERC's reform meaningfully shorten permitting? How wide is the gap between headline forecasts and achievable demand?

**Why now:** Williams, Kinder Morgan and Energy Transfer's Q2 2026 earnings (late July-early August) all disclosed record data-center-driven gas-infrastructure activity in the same window, while FERC's May 21, 2026 blanket-certificate overhaul -- its first in 20 years -- is a direct regulatory response to a pre-existing multi-year permitting backlog. This midstream transport layer is distinct from the gas-turbine bottleneck (2026-09-16) and the electric-grid interconnection bottlenecks (PJM BYONC, 2026-08-28; ghost demand, 2026-09-06) this pipeline has already examined.

**Thesis:** Long-haul, FERC-certificated interstate gas pipeline capacity meets the strict structural-bottleneck test, while capital-light laterals and behind-the-meter gas supply remain a faster-moving Growth Area; FERC's own 2026 reform push is itself evidence the permitting layer, not physical construction ability, is the binding constraint.

# Reasonable Inferences

- All three midstream operators are raising backlogs on the same data-center demand while flagship interstate expansions still take until 2028-2030, indicating near-term certificated capacity is running behind disclosed demand at that layer specifically. _(s1, s2, s3, s6)_
- FERC's unanimous, first-in-20-years rulemaking indicates regulators view permitting delay, not construction capability, as the binding constraint on gas infrastructure for the AI buildout. _(s5, s9, s10)_
- Faster in-service timelines for laterals/behind-the-meter projects versus multi-year mainline expansions show elasticity is bifurcated by project type, echoing bottleneck/Growth-Area splits seen elsewhere in this pipeline's research. _(s3, s6, s8)_
- Wide dispersion in gas-demand forecasts (3-20 Bcf/d) plus East Daley's self-discounting suggests true demand magnitude is not yet measurable with confidence, echoing the "ghost demand" pattern in electric interconnection. _(s7, s12)_

# Core Mechanism

Every new gas-fired power plant serving an AI data center needs firm, contracted gas transport, and for large, non-behind-the-meter loads that increasingly means new or expanded interstate pipeline capacity requiring FERC certification. Williams, Kinder Morgan and Energy Transfer all reported record 2026 backlogs and raised long-term guidance on explicit data-center demand within the same earnings window, while a pre-existing FERC certificate backlog (15 projects, 465-day average) and multi-year in-service dates for flagship expansions -- Power Express not online until July 2030 -- show that capacity is not simply a matter of writing a check.

That combination is the signature of Low near-term supply elasticity at the certificated long-haul layer specifically. But the response is real and bifurcated: laterals and behind-the-meter projects that bypass FERC review (Atlas, Socrates, the Abilene lateral) have shipped in a single quarter to under two years, and FERC's own unanimous 2026 rulemaking is a direct, bipartisan attempt to shorten the certificate process that gates the slower-moving mainline layer.

# Strongest Bear Case

- **Supply**: All three operators are expanding via M&A, mainline projects and fast laterals, and FERC's unanimous reform targets the constraint directly -- this could be a temporary lag, not a durable ceiling.
- **Demand**: Analyst estimates span 3-20 Bcf/d by 2030, and East Daley discounts its own forecast, echoing the "ghost demand" overstatement pattern seen in electric interconnection.
- **Economics**: Incremental pipeline capex is comparatively modest (NGPL's Amarillo Expansion: ~$200M for 0.55 Bcf/d), and laterals have shipped in a single quarter.
- **Regulation**: FERC's 5-0 vote to overhaul blanket certification directly targets the identified permitting bottleneck.
- **Technology**: Behind-the-meter generation and (per this pipeline's HALEU and battery reports) nuclear/battery alternatives could reduce reliance on long-haul gas transport specifically.

# Final Assessment

**Verdict: VALIDATE** (confidence: medium)

Long-haul interstate gas pipeline transport meets the strict structural-bottleneck test: extreme demand, low near-term elasticity evidenced by a pre-reform 465-day certificate backlog and multi-year in-service dates even for fully subscribed expansions, and hard substitutability because firm gas transport has no ready near-term alternative at hyperscale. But laterals, behind-the-meter projects and gas gathering each fail at least one leg of the test, and FERC's own unanimous 2026 rulemaking is direct evidence regulators see the permitting layer -- not physical construction ability -- as the binding constraint. No hyperscaler has yet blamed a delay on pipeline capacity specifically, and analyst disagreement on real incremental demand (3-20 Bcf/d) is wide. VALIDATE, not BUILD NOW or PAUSE: Q3 2026 earnings and FERC's rulemaking progress should show whether the permitting reform and fast-moving laterals close the gap before mainline capacity does.
