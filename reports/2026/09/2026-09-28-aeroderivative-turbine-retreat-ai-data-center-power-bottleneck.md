# 一句話結論

Crusoe's collapsed $1.25 billion Boom Supersonic turbine deal looks like a company-specific retreat, not a bottleneck fix -- heavy-frame OEM order books remain untouched.

# Executive Summary

On September 25, 2026, Boom Supersonic confirmed Crusoe -- its sole disclosed launch customer -- will not take delivery of the 29 aeroderivative turbines ($1.25B, ~1.21GW) it ordered in December 2025 to sidestep heavy-frame OEMs' multi-year lead times. It is Crusoe's second infrastructure retreat in three months, after a June exit from a 1.8GW Wyoming campus over Google's cost and schedule concerns, and comes eight days after Crusoe's record $3.9B Series F at a $30.9B valuation. Boom still guides to 250MW (2027) and 1GW (2028) of deliveries to other, unnamed customers, suggesting the product itself has not failed. But that pipeline remains two to three orders of magnitude smaller than GE Vernova's, Siemens Energy's and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries' combined roughly 220GW order books, which show no sign of easing. The event reads as company-specific capital discipline, not a resolution of the gas turbine bottleneck this pipeline identified September 16.

**Primary question:** see front matter above.

**Sub-questions:**

- Does Crusoe's pattern of retreats despite record funding say more about capital discipline than about power-hardware supply?
- What does Google's reported role in the Wyoming exit suggest about hyperscaler risk tolerance for newer power technology?

**Why now:** Boom Supersonic's CEO confirmed the Crusoe cancellation September 25, 2026, three months after Crusoe's Wyoming exit and eight days after its record Series F -- a concrete test of this pipeline's most recent power-bottleneck thesis.

**Thesis:** Crusoe's cancellation looks like a company-specific capital-allocation retreat rather than a failure of aeroderivative turbines as technology, but because Boom's entire near-term pipeline remains far smaller than the heavy-frame OEMs' order books, the event reinforces rather than weakens the original bottleneck finding.

# Core Mechanism

Heavy-frame gas turbines from four OEMs face 3-6 year lead times against roughly 110GW/year of orders and only 60-70GW/year of manufacturing capacity. Aeroderivative turbines -- jet-engine-derived designs like Boom's Superpower -- were pitched as a faster workaround, using existing aviation supply chains rather than the specialized forging and casting capacity heavy-frame turbines require. Crusoe's December 2025 order was the AI industry's largest disclosed bet on that workaround.

Its September 2026 cancellation, paired with Crusoe's June exit from a Wyoming campus after a hyperscaler's cost and schedule concerns, and a record funding round eight days earlier, points to a company pulling back from large fixed commitments -- not aeroderivative turbines failing as technology. Boom's continued guidance to other customers supports that reading. But because Boom's near-term volume (250MW-1GW) is far below the heavy-frame OEMs' combined ~220GW backlog, the workaround was never large enough to relieve the bottleneck even before Crusoe exited, so the heavy-frame constraint persists regardless of this event's cause.

# Final Assessment

**Verdict: VALIDATE** (confidence: medium)

This event neither confirms nor breaks the September 16, 2026 Structural Bottleneck classification for heavy-frame gas turbines -- it reinforces it by elimination. The one prominent attempt to bypass heavy-frame OEMs at meaningful scale, Crusoe's 1.21GW Boom order, has been cancelled, and the alternative -- Boom's continuing but far smaller pipeline -- was never close to matching the ~220GW heavy-frame backlog. Absent evidence the heavy-frame OEMs' own order books have softened, the bottleneck stands. The open question is attribution: whether this is Crusoe-specific capital discipline or an early signal of technology risk in aeroderivative turbines, which only Boom landing, or failing to land, a new customer will resolve.
